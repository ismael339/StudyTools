How to Study for a Math Test and Get an A

Math is not memorised, it is practised. This guide covers the exact revision plan that works: what to study, in what order, how to practise without getting stuck, and what to do in the final hour before the test.

Why Math Tests Are Different From Other Subjects

In history or biology you can often get marks by recalling a fact in the right order. In maths, a correct answer with no working usually scores nothing, and a wrong answer with correct method scores partial marks. That single fact changes how you should revise.

Maths needs two separate skills: conceptual understanding, knowing why a method exists, and procedural fluency, being able to execute it without hesitation. Most students practise only the second. They can grind through the steps of a familiar type of question but freeze when the exam words it differently, because they never understood the underlying idea.

Step 1: Build a Topic List From the Syllabus, Not From the Textbook

Open your course syllabus or the list of topics the teacher has covered. Turn each item into a checklist. This gives you three things at once: an honest picture of the scope, a way to spot the topics you have never touched, and a clear definition of done.

Sort every topic into one of three groups on day one, before you revise anything:

Green: you can do these unaided

You can solve a standard problem start to finish without notes. Keep these warm, do not spend your time here.

Amber: you understand it but you are slow or forgetful

You get there eventually, or you need the formula sheet in front of you. This is where most of your marks are, because the concepts are already in place.

Red: you do not understand it

You cannot explain why the method works. This needs proper attention early, not the night before.

Step 2: Fix the Red Topics First, and Understand Before You Practise

For each red topic, do not start with twenty practice questions. Start with one worked example and follow it with your finger, line by line, asking at every step: why am I doing this now, and what would happen if I did it differently?

Then close the example and try to reproduce it on a blank page. If you cannot, you have read rather than learned. This is the fastest way to find out whether you actually understand something, and it takes ten minutes instead of an hour of passive review.

Write down the one-sentence version of the idea. If you cannot explain a method in a single sentence without using jargon, you do not understand it yet, and no amount of practice questions will fix that.

Step 3: Practise Problems in a Deliberate Order

Once a topic makes sense, practise it deliberately. The order matters more than the volume.

  • Start easy and build. Three simple problems, then medium, then the hardest type. Success builds the confidence to attempt the hard ones.
  • Do the hard ones without notes. If the topic is in your head, the practice has to be without help, otherwise you are only testing whether you can follow instructions.
  • Time yourself from the start. Exam pace is a skill. A problem that takes you twelve minutes when it should take four is costing you marks on the questions after it.
  • Mark honestly. Give yourself the marks your teacher would give. Partial credit counts, and noticing that you lose marks on a step you skipped is exactly what you need to fix.

Step 4: Keep an Error Log, Which Is the Highest-Value Part of Revision

Every mistake you make, write down: the question, what you did, what went wrong, and the correct approach. Keep it in one place and review it before every session.

This is far more valuable than redoing the same problems until they feel easy, which is the most common and most useless form of maths revision. Problems you already solved correctly teach you little. The ones you got wrong contain every single mark you are about to lose in the exam, because the same misunderstanding will appear again under exam pressure.

Aim for a small error log you actually re-read, ten to fifteen entries, rather than a large one you never open.

Step 5: Learn When to Stop and Move On

Getting stuck is normal and it is not a reason to abandon the question. But there is a point where more time on one problem costs you more than it gains.

Use this rule: if you have been stuck for more than ten minutes and you cannot make progress, stop. Write down exactly where you got stuck, mark the question to return to, and move to the next one. Come back to it at the end of the session with fresh eyes. Problems that defeat you at minute ten are often solved in two minutes after a break, because the mental block was fatigue rather than difficulty.

Step 6: The Formula Sheet Is a Memory Test, Not a Writing Exercise

Students rewrite their formula sheets three times and call it revision. Rewriting builds familiarity, which feels like knowledge, but it does not build recall.

What works is retrieval: write the sheet from memory, then check what you missed. Blank sheet, pen, five minutes, then compare. The gaps you find are exactly the formulas that will not arrive under pressure in the exam hall. If you have twenty minutes, do this test three times with a ten-minute gap rather than copying the list once.

Step 7: The Final 24 Hours

Do not start a new topic. The returns on a topic you have never touched are minimal and the stress cost is high.

Instead, in order of value: re-read your error log and fix the causes, run three or four problems from your weakest topic, do one short past paper under real time conditions, and then read your formula sheet twice. Stop studying at least a few hours before you sleep, because the consolidation that turns practice into memory happens during sleep, not during a last-minute session.

In the Exam Itself

Read every instruction before writing anything, including the marks. Questions are usually worth roughly one mark per step, which tells you how much working to show. Write each step as its own line, because a crossed-out error loses a method mark less often than a single long line where a slip looks final.

If you get stuck, do not leave it blank. Write the formula, the substitution, the first line of working, or sketch the diagram. Partial marks are real marks, and something written is something that can be credited. If you blank completely, move on immediately and come back, because one hard question can quietly cost you three easy ones you never reached.

Check your units and your signs in the last two minutes. Lost minus signs and wrong units are among the most common reasons students lose a mark on work they actually understood.

Frequently Asked Questions

How far in advance should I start studying for a maths test?

One to two weeks is the useful range. Starting the day before gives you cramming, which builds almost no long-term memory. A short session every second day from a week out is far more effective than three hours the night before.

How many practice questions should I do?

Fewer, better questions than many careless ones. Ten problems done slowly, marked honestly and logged beats fifty done at speed to see the answer. Quality of feedback is what converts practice into skill.

Should I memorise formulas for a maths test?

Memorise the small number you use constantly, and understand the rest. Understanding why a formula holds means you can reconstruct it, adapt it and spot when it does not apply, which is what exam questions test.

Is it better to study maths alone or with others?

Both, for different purposes. Practising alone under time pressure is what builds exam skill. Explaining a problem out loud to someone else is the fastest way to find the gaps in your understanding, because you discover exactly where you cannot continue.

What if I only get C grades in maths?

Then the highest-value move is a topic-by-topic diagnosis. Most students with a C have one or two weak areas holding the whole grade down, not a uniform lack of ability. Fixing those two topics is a realistic route from C to B.
