Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between APA, MLA, and Chicago?
APA (American Psychological Association) is used mainly in social sciences and psychology. MLA (Modern Language Association) is common in humanities and literature. Chicago style is widely used in history. Your professor or assignment instructions will specify which one to use.
How do I cite a website in APA format?
APA website format: Author, A. A. (Year, Month Day). Title of page. Site Name. URL. If no author, start with the title. If no date, write (n.d.). Example: Smith, J. (2023, March 15). How to study effectively. StudyTools. https://www.studytools.pro
How do I cite a book in MLA format?
MLA book format: Last, First. Title of Book. Publisher, Year. Example: Orwell, George. 1984. Secker and Warburg, 1949. For multiple authors, list them all in the order they appear on the title page.
Is this citation generator accurate?
This tool follows APA 7th edition, MLA 9th edition, and Chicago 17th edition guidelines. However, always review citations against your style guide or check with your professor — requirements can vary by institution.
What if some information is missing?
Leave blank fields empty — the generator will skip them. APA uses "n.d." for no date, and skips the author field if unknown. Always try to find missing information directly on the source before leaving it blank.
Do I need a citation if I put it in my own words?
Yes. Paraphrasing changes the wording, not the ownership of the idea. Any fact, statistic, argument or interpretation that is not common knowledge needs a citation, even without quotation marks. The rule of thumb: if you learned it from a source rather than knowing it already, cite it.
How do I cite ChatGPT or another AI tool?
APA 7 treats it as software: OpenAI. (2024). ChatGPT (version) [Large language model]. URL. Many departments also ask you to put the exact prompt in an appendix, and some prohibit AI-assisted work entirely — read your assessment policy before you cite it.
What is the difference between a reference list and a bibliography?
A reference list contains only the sources you actually cited in the text — that is what APA and MLA require. A bibliography can include background reading you consulted but never cited, which is common in Chicago notes-and-bibliography style. Mixing them up is a quick way to lose formatting marks.

How to Cite Sources in APA, MLA and Chicago (Without Losing Marks)

Citations are the easiest marks on any assignment and the ones students lose most often. The rules are mechanical: a fixed order of elements, a fixed punctuation pattern, a fixed way of pointing from the sentence in your text to the entry in your reference list. Nothing about it requires thought — which is precisely why it is worth automating with the generator above and spending the saved hour on the argument instead. This guide explains what the generator produces, so you can check its output rather than trust it blindly.

How to use the generator

  1. Pick your style — APA 7, MLA 9 or Chicago 17. If you are not sure, the assignment brief or module handbook says so; ask before guessing, because converting an entire reference list at midnight is miserable.
  2. Pick the source type — book, journal article, website. This is what determines which elements are required and in what order.
  3. Fill in what you have. Leave unknown fields blank; the generator omits them and applies the correct no-author/no-date convention.
  4. Copy the formatted citation into your reference list, and use the matching in-text form in the sentence itself.
  5. Save as you go. Citations you generate stay in the saved list on this page, so you can collect sources while you read and export the whole list at the end.

The three styles at a glance

StyleUsed inIn-textList title
APA 7Psychology, education, social sciences, nursing(Smith, 2023) or (Smith, 2023, p. 14)References
MLA 9Literature, languages, cultural studies, humanities(Smith 14)Works Cited
Chicago 17History, art history, some business schoolsFootnote 1 or (Smith 2023, 14)Bibliography

The differences look small and are not: APA uses sentence case for article titles and initials for first names, MLA uses title case and full first names, and Chicago's notes system puts the full detail in a footnote the first time a source appears. Getting this consistently right by hand across thirty sources is where the hour disappears.

Worked examples

A book, all three ways:

  • APA: Kahneman, D. (2011). Thinking, fast and slow. Farrar, Straus and Giroux.
  • MLA: Kahneman, Daniel. Thinking, Fast and Slow. Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 2011.
  • Chicago (bibliography): Kahneman, Daniel. Thinking, Fast and Slow. New York: Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 2011.

A journal article in APA: Roediger, H. L., & Karpicke, J. D. (2006). Test-enhanced learning. Psychological Science, 17(3), 249–255. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x — note the volume in italics, the issue in brackets, and the DOI as a full link with no full stop after it.

A website with no author in MLA: "How to Study Effectively." StudyTools, 2026, www.studytools.pro. The title moves into the author slot, and the in-text citation becomes a shortened version of that title.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

  • Mixing styles in one document. Half-APA, half-MLA is the single most common formatting penalty. Pick one and run a find-check on the pattern before submitting.
  • Citing the database instead of the source. JSTOR and ProQuest are where you found the article, not who published it. Cite the journal.
  • Missing page numbers on direct quotes. APA and MLA both require them for quotations; leaving them out edges towards a plagiarism flag rather than a formatting slip.
  • Broken alphabetical order. Reference lists are ordered by author surname, not by the order you cited them. Sort once at the end.
  • Uncited paraphrase. Rewording still requires a citation. Only genuinely common knowledge is exempt.
  • Sources in the list that never appear in the text. In APA and MLA every entry must be cited somewhere. Reading list ≠ reference list.
  • Blind trust in any generator, including this one. Automated tools reproduce whatever metadata you give them. Check capitalisation, italics and the author order against the source once before you submit.

Advanced tips

Cite while you read, not after you write. Generate and save the entry the moment you use a source. Reconstructing twenty references from browser history at 2 a.m. is how details get invented.

Copy the DOI, not the search URL. A DOI is permanent; a session URL from a library proxy breaks within days and looks careless.

Keep a one-line note with each saved citation — what the source argues and which of your points it supports. It turns a reference list into an outline.

Check your word count rules. Reference lists are usually excluded from the limit but in-text citations usually are not; the word counter lets you check the body text on its own.

Timebox the formatting. Citations are a mechanical task, so give them one 25-minute block with the Pomodoro timer rather than letting them leak across the evening.

Ask when a source is unusual. Podcasts, lecture slides, datasets and interviews all have specific patterns; the AI tutor can walk you through the elements, and your library's style guide is the final authority.
