Exam anxiety: how to calm down and still perform

Feeling sick before an exam is extremely common and almost always manageable. This is about what is happening in your body, what to do about it the night before, and what to do in the exam room when your mind goes blank.

What exam anxiety actually is

An exam is a threat your body takes seriously, whether or not it is rational. The stress response releases adrenaline and cortisol, which is useful for short periods and unhelpful for days. Your heart races, your mouth goes dry, you cannot sleep, and your hands shake when you pick up the pen.

That physical reaction is the problem, not the thinking. A little of it is normal and even useful: moderate arousal improves performance. It becomes a difficulty when it is strong enough to disrupt your sleep, your eating, your revision, or the exam itself.

Useful reframe: you are not "bad at exams", you have a body that responds strongly to pressure. That is trainable, and the physical side responds faster than you would expect.

The night before: what actually helps

Most of the suffering the night before comes from trying to force yourself to feel calm, and from rereading notes to feel reassured. Both backfire. Rereading highlights what you do not know, which raises anxiety, and forcing calm usually fails because it is an instruction your body cannot obey.

Do short, specific retrieval instead

Instead of rereading, test yourself on three topics you know well. Write out a formula sheet from memory, answer five questions you have already solved, explain a topic out loud. The point is to give your brain evidence that you do know things, which is far more calming than reassurance.

Make the task concrete

Anxiety grows when the task is vague. Write down exactly what you will do tomorrow: what time you will leave, what you will eat, what you will review while waiting. Deciding in advance removes the mental load of deciding later.

Do something physical

A 20-minute walk, a few stretches, or a warm shower reduces physiological arousal faster than sitting still and trying to think calm thoughts. Movement burns off the adrenaline your body is producing.

Stop early, on purpose

Set a firm stopping time and keep it. Revising until 2am gives you more content and far less recall, because consolidation happens during sleep. Going to bed early is a technique, not surrendering.

In the exam room, when you blank

This is the moment most students fear, and it has a simple answer. When recall fails completely, write down anything related: the formula, the diagram, the first step of the method, a worked example from your notes. Recall frequently returns once you start writing, because writing is itself a retrieval cue.

If nothing comes back, move to the next question immediately. Partial credit is real credit, and one hard question left unfinished can quietly cost you three easy ones you never reached. Answer what you can and come back at the end if time allows.

Read every instruction first. Under pressure, students lose marks on the mechanics: wrong question count, wrong command words, unanswered sections. Two minutes of reading prevents that.

Also do the easy questions first. Securing the marks you know builds momentum and stops the panic spiral where one hard question triggers a full blank.

When the panic comes mid-session

It can hit without warning: tunnel vision, shaking, the sense that everyone is writing and you are the only one stuck. The physiological reset that works fastest is slow breathing, because it is the one part of the nervous system you can deliberately slow down.

Breathe in for four counts, hold for four, out for six. The long exhale is the active ingredient, because it is the part your body cannot ignore. Three or four cycles, then return to the question in front of you. You do not need to feel completely calm to keep going; you need to keep going while calming.

Stopping the catastrophising about results

A lot of exam anxiety is not about the exam, it is about the imagined future: failing, disappointing people, the wrong course, wasted years. That chain is catastrophising, and it is worth arguing with directly.

Ask what evidence you actually have. Students consistently predict their results far worse than they turn out, because a bad hour in an exam room feels far more significant from the inside than it does from the results sheet. The feeling is a poor estimator of the outcome.

And separate what you control from what you do not. You control the sessions this week. You do not control the grade, the marker, the other candidates, or what happens next year. Spending attention on the first kind is productive; spending it on the second is the anxiety itself.

When to get more help

If anxiety is stopping you sleeping, eating, attending, or revising for more than a couple of weeks, that is beyond exam nerves and worth talking to someone about: a tutor, a school counsellor, or your doctor. Support exists for this and asking is a sign of strength, not weakness.

It is also worth telling someone if the pressure comes from a situation rather than the exam itself, because that is a different problem with a different fix.

Frequently asked questions

Is exam anxiety normal?

Yes. A racing heart before an exam is a normal stress response, and mild anxiety can even sharpen focus. It becomes a problem when it disrupts sleep, eating, revision, or the exam itself.

How do I stop feeling sick before an exam?

The physical symptoms come from the stress response, so they ease as the stress does. Slow breathing with a long exhale, a short walk, eating something small, and two minutes of retrieval on a topic you know all help more than trying to force yourself to relax.

What do I do if I blank during an exam?

Write down anything related: a formula, a diagram, the first step. Recall often returns once you start writing. Then move to the next question and come back, rather than letting one hard question take the whole exam.

How do I stop catastrophising about results?

Ask what evidence you actually have, and focus on actions you control this week rather than outcomes you do not. Most students predict their results far worse than they turn out to be.

Should I revise more to calm down?

Rereading to feel reassured usually raises anxiety, because it highlights gaps. Short retrieval sessions on topics you already know are more calming, because they give your brain evidence that you do know things.
