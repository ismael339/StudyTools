Frequently Asked Questions

How is GPA calculated?
GPA is calculated by multiplying the grade points of each course by the number of credit hours, summing all those values, then dividing by the total credit hours. For example, an A (4.0) in a 3-credit course gives 12 quality points. Divide total quality points by total credits to get your GPA.
What is a good GPA?
A GPA of 3.0 or higher is generally considered good at the college level. A 3.5 or above is considered excellent and qualifies for academic honors. For competitive graduate school programs, a 3.5+ is usually expected.
What is the difference between weighted and unweighted GPA?
An unweighted GPA uses a standard 4.0 scale regardless of course difficulty. A weighted GPA gives extra points for harder courses like AP or IB classes, often on a 5.0 scale. Most college applications use the unweighted 4.0 scale.
How do I calculate my cumulative GPA?
Use the Cumulative tab above. Enter your current GPA and total credit hours earned so far, then add your new semester's courses and grades. The calculator will weigh everything correctly and show your updated cumulative GPA.
Does this work for both high school and college?
Yes. This calculator uses the standard 4.0 scale used by the vast majority of US high schools and colleges. The calculation method works for both levels.
What percentage is a 3.5 GPA?
A 3.5 GPA sits between a B+ and an A− average, which is roughly 88–90% on a typical US grading scale. Exact cut-offs vary by institution, so check your syllabus: an 89% is an A− at some schools and a B+ at others, and that single boundary is often what separates a 3.3 from a 3.7 in a four-credit course.
How much can one bad grade drop my GPA?
It depends on how many credits you have already earned. With 15 credits banked, a C in a 3-credit course can pull a 3.8 down by about two tenths. With 90 credits banked, the same C moves your cumulative GPA by roughly three hundredths. Early grades are the ones that really matter — and the ones that are cheapest to repair.
Do pass/fail and withdrawn courses count toward GPA?
Normally no. A P or a W carries no grade points and no credit hours in the GPA calculation, so leave those courses out of the calculator. They can still affect financial aid and satisfactory-progress rules, which are tracked separately from GPA, so check your school's policy before withdrawing.
Can I raise my GPA from 2.5 to 3.0 in one semester?
Only if you are early in your degree. With 30 credits at 2.5, a 15-credit semester at 4.0 puts you at exactly 3.0. With 60 credits already earned, the same perfect semester only reaches 2.8, and you would need two strong semesters. Use the Cumulative tab above to model your own numbers before setting a target.
Is my data saved anywhere?
No. Every calculation runs in your browser. Nothing is uploaded, no account is required, and closing the tab clears the courses you entered.

How to Calculate Your GPA (and What the Number Actually Means)

Most students look up a GPA calculator for one of three reasons: they want to know their GPA right now, they want to know what GPA they will end up with if a specific semester goes well or badly, or someone told them a number — 3.5, 3.0, 2.5 — and they need to know whether that is good. This page answers all three. The calculator above gives you the number in a few seconds, and the guide below explains how the number is built, how to move it, and how admissions offices and scholarship committees actually read it.

Grade point average is a credit-weighted average. That single word, weighted, is where nearly every hand calculation goes wrong. Your GPA is not the average of your letter grades. A course worth 4 credits pulls roughly twice as hard on your average as a course worth 2 credits, so an A in a one-credit lab does far less for you than an A in a four-credit core class — and a C in that four-credit class hurts correspondingly more.

The formula, step by step

The calculation has three steps, and the calculator above does all of them for you:

  1. Convert each letter grade to grade points using the 4.0 scale (A = 4.0, A− = 3.7, B+ = 3.3, and so on — the full table is above).
  2. Multiply each course's grade points by its credit hours. The result is called quality points. An A− (3.7) in a 3-credit course is 11.1 quality points.
  3. Divide total quality points by total credit hours. That is your GPA.

Written as a formula: GPA = Σ(grade points × credits) ÷ Σ(credits).

49.5 ÷ 15 = 3.30 GPA. Notice what the table makes obvious: the two four-credit science and maths courses supply 22.4 of the 49.5 quality points. Lifting that C+ in Calculus to a B− would add 1.6 quality points and move the semester to 3.41. Lifting the one-credit lab from A to A+ would change nothing at all, because A+ and A are both 4.0 on most scales. That is the whole game — effort spent on high-credit courses moves your GPA, effort spent on low-credit courses mostly does not.

Cumulative GPA: adding a semester to the ones you already have

Your cumulative GPA is the same calculation run over every course you have ever taken, which is why a single semester moves it less and less as you progress. Use the Cumulative tab above: enter your current GPA and the total credits you have already earned, then add the new semester's courses. Behind the scenes the calculator converts your existing record back into quality points (current GPA × credits earned) and folds the new courses in.

The practical consequence surprises people. A student with a 2.8 GPA over 60 credits who earns a perfect 4.0 across a 15-credit semester finishes at 3.04, not 3.2. With 90 credits already banked, that same perfect semester only reaches 2.97. Early semesters are cheap to fix and late semesters are expensive, so if your GPA matters for an application two years out, the time to act is the semester you are in now.

Weighted vs unweighted GPA

An unweighted GPA caps every course at 4.0 no matter how hard it is. A weighted GPA adds a bonus — typically +0.5 for honours and +1.0 for AP or IB — so a B in AP Chemistry can be worth as much as an A in a standard class, and averages above 4.0 become possible. High schools report weighted GPAs because they reward students for taking difficult courses; many universities then recalculate an unweighted core GPA from your transcript so applicants from different schools can be compared fairly.

What that means for you: know both numbers. Use the calculator above for your unweighted 4.0 GPA, then add your school's bonus per honours/AP course for the weighted figure. If you are choosing between an easy A and a hard B in an AP course, the hard B is usually the better application — but only if it does not drag two other courses down with it.

What counts as a good GPA

  • 3.7–4.0 — competitive for selective universities, honours programmes and most merit scholarships.
  • 3.3–3.7 — strong. Comfortably above the bar at most institutions and graduate programmes.
  • 3.0–3.3 — solid, and the threshold many scholarships, internships and study-abroad programmes use as a minimum.
  • 2.0–3.0 — passing, but likely to limit options. Worth a deliberate recovery plan.
  • Below 2.0 — most institutions trigger academic probation here; talk to an adviser early rather than late.

Context matters more than the raw number. A 3.4 in engineering with a heavy laboratory load reads differently from a 3.4 in a light schedule, and an upward trend across four semesters reads far better than a flat 3.6 that started at 3.9. Admissions readers look at the shape of the transcript, not just the final average.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

  • Averaging letter grades directly. Adding 4.0 + 3.0 + 2.0 and dividing by three ignores credits and can be off by two or three tenths. Always weight by credit hours.
  • Forgetting pass/fail and withdrawn courses. A P or W normally carries no grade points and no credits toward the GPA — but it usually still counts against completion-rate rules for financial aid. Leave them out of the calculator, and check your aid terms separately.
  • Assuming A+ is 4.3. A minority of institutions award 4.3 for A+; most cap at 4.0. Check your registrar's scale before you promise yourself a 4.1.
  • Using percentage cut-offs from another school. An 89% is a B+ at one institution and an A− at another. Use your own syllabus's cut-offs when converting.
  • Ignoring retake policies. Many schools replace the original grade when you retake a failed course; others average both attempts. The difference can be worth a full tenth of a point.
  • Planning with wishful grades. When you model next semester, enter the grade you are currently earning, not the one you hope to earn. Then use the gap as motivation.

Advanced tips for raising your GPA

Target the highest-credit course you are weakest in. Run the calculator twice — once with your realistic grades, once with one grade improved — and you will see immediately which course is worth your Sunday afternoon.

Fix the calendar before you fix the studying. Most GPA damage is caused by two deadlines colliding, not by difficulty. Map the whole term with the study schedule planner, then protect the two weeks before finals.

Convert reading into retrieval. Rereading feels productive and produces very little. Turn each chapter into questions with the flashcard maker, then test yourself instead of reviewing. If it feels boring, run the cards as a 30-second timed round in PLAY — same retrieval practice, far more likely to actually happen.

Work in fixed blocks. Two focused 25-minute blocks with the Pomodoro timer beat three distracted hours; the difference shows up in mid-terms.

Know the exact score you need. Before every final, run the grade calculator so you know the minimum mark that protects your target. Studying without that number is how students over-prepare for a class that was already safe and under-prepare for the one that was not.

Ask earlier than feels comfortable. Office hours in week three change grades; office hours in week thirteen change very little. If a concept is unclear, get it explained the same week — the AI tutor is there for the 11 p.m. version of that question.
