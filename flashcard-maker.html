Frequently Asked Questions

How do flashcards help you study?
Flashcards work through active recall — instead of passively reading notes, you force your brain to retrieve information. Combined with spaced repetition (reviewing cards you struggle with more often), flashcards are one of the most evidence-backed study methods available.
What should I put on flashcards?
Keep each card focused on one concept. Good candidates: vocabulary definitions, historical dates, math formulas, scientific terms, foreign language words, grammar rules, and key facts. Avoid putting too much text on a single card — if it needs more than 2-3 sentences, split it into multiple cards.
Are my flashcards saved?
Your deck is saved automatically in your browser, so it is still there when you come back on the same device. Nothing is uploaded to a server. Your saved deck is also playable in Flashcard Battle.
How many flashcards should I make per topic?
Twenty to forty cards per lecture or textbook chapter is the usual sweet spot. Below twenty you are probably cramming several ideas onto one card; above sixty you are copying the text instead of selecting what matters. One testable idea per card is the standard to hold yourself to.
How long does it take to memorize 100 flashcards?
With spaced repetition, most students reach reliable recall of 100 simple cards in five to seven short sessions across one or two weeks — about 15–20 minutes a day. The same 100 cards crammed in one evening will feel learned that night and be largely gone within 48 hours.
Are digital flashcards better than paper ones?
Digital cards are faster to make, impossible to lose, easy to shuffle and can be turned into a timed game. Paper adds a small handwriting benefit and zero distractions. Either beats the other by a mile if you answer out loud before flipping — and loses if you do not.
Can I use flashcards for maths and formulas?
Yes, but change what goes on the front. Instead of "Quadratic formula → x = (−b ± √(b²−4ac)) / 2a", put a small worked problem on the front and the solved steps on the back. Memorising a formula you cannot apply under time pressure earns no marks.

How to Make Flashcards That Actually Work

Flashcards are the most misused study tool in existence. Most students make a hundred of them, flip through the stack twice, recognise every answer, feel prepared, and then blank in the exam. The cards were not the problem — the way they were written and reviewed was. This guide covers what to put on a card, how many to make, how to review them so the material actually sticks, and the specific mistakes that turn an hour of card-making into wasted time.

The underlying principle is active recall: retrieving an answer from memory strengthens that memory far more than seeing it again does. Rereading a chapter feels productive because it feels easy. Answering a question before you flip feels harder — and that difficulty is exactly what makes it work. Decades of cognitive-psychology research on the testing effect point in the same direction: students who self-test outperform students who reread, even when the rereaders spend more total time.

How to use this flashcard maker

  1. Type the question on the front and the answer on the back. Front should be a genuine prompt, not a topic heading.
  2. Add cards as you read. Making cards while working through a chapter forces you to decide what is testable, which is half the learning.
  3. Study the deck. Say your answer out loud before flipping. If you flip first, you are reading, not recalling.
  4. Be honest about misses. "I nearly had it" is a miss. Nearly does not score marks.
  5. Come back tomorrow. Your deck is saved in your browser, so the same cards are waiting. Spacing the reviews is where the memory is built.
  6. Turn it into a game. Your saved deck feeds straight into Flashcard Battle in PLAY — a 30-second timed round with scoring and streaks, which is the easiest way to make daily review something you actually do.

The single best test of a card: could you answer it correctly while knowing almost nothing about the topic? If yes — because the answer is obvious from the wording, or because it is always "true" — rewrite it.

A practical example: one biology chapter

Say you have a chapter on cellular respiration and an exam in ten days. Read it once with the card maker open. Every time the text states something you could be asked about, make a card: "Where does glycolysis take place?", "How many ATP does glycolysis net produce?", "Why does the Krebs cycle stop without oxygen?". That is roughly 30 cards for a dense chapter.

Then review on a schedule, not on a mood: day 1 all 30 cards, day 2 only the ones you missed plus a sample of the rest, day 4 the whole deck again, day 7 the whole deck, day 10 the misses. Total time invested: under two hours. Compare it with three hours of highlighting, which produces a colourful chapter and no evidence you know anything.

For language vocabulary the same structure applies, with one addition: make cards in both directions. Recognising "le bâtiment" means building is easy; producing "bâtiment" when prompted with "building" is what you need in a writing exam.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

  • Flipping too early. The three seconds of struggle before the answer arrives is the entire mechanism. Wait them out.
  • Cards that are copied sentences. If the back has more than about 20 words, split the card.
  • Only reviewing the deck in the order you made it. You end up learning the sequence, not the content. Shuffle — or use the timed random rounds in PLAY.
  • Dropping cards as soon as you get them right once. One correct answer is not learning; it is a coin flip you happened to win. Retire a card after three correct recalls on three different days.
  • Making the entire deck the night before. Card-making is not studying. If cards and exam are the same night, you have bought yourself a well-organised panic.
  • Ignoring the cards you hate. The cards you keep skipping are precisely the material that will appear in the exam. Start each session with the five you got wrong last time.
  • Studying with the answers visible. Recognition feels like knowledge and is not. Cover, answer, then check.

Advanced tips

Use spaced intervals, not daily grinding. Review at 1 day, 3 days, 7 days and 14 days. Expanding intervals produce dramatically better long-term retention than the same total time spent in consecutive days.

Interleave subjects. Mixing chemistry and maths cards in one session feels worse and tests better, because exams do not arrive topic by topic.

Add a "why" card for every "what" card. Definitions get you passing marks; explanations get you the top band.

Write cards in your own words. The act of rephrasing is itself a comprehension check — if you cannot rephrase it, you have found a gap.

Attach cards to a timer. Twenty-five focused minutes with the Pomodoro timer beats an unfocused hour, and card review fits a single block almost perfectly.

Schedule the reviews, do not remember them. Put the day-3 and day-7 passes in the study schedule maker the moment you build the deck.

Get unstuck instead of guessing. When a card keeps failing, the problem is usually understanding, not memory — ask the AI study assistant to explain the concept, then rewrite the card.

How this compares with the alternatives

Versus Quizlet: Quizlet has huge shared deck libraries and more study modes, but pushes you towards an account, adverts and a subscription for its better features. Shared decks are also a trap — someone else's cards skip the most valuable part, which is deciding what matters.

Versus Anki: Anki's scheduling algorithm is the best there is for long-term retention, and its learning curve and interface put most students off within a week. This tool is the fast entry point: no install, no account, working deck in five minutes.

Versus paper cards: paper wins on distraction-free focus and the small memory benefit of handwriting. It loses on speed, shuffling, and the fact that a paper deck cannot be turned into a timed scoring game.

Versus rereading your notes: not a real contest. Rereading is the most popular study method and one of the least effective; retrieval practice is the opposite on both counts.
